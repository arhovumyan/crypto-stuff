Here’s my specific plan and the approach I would take to build your “buy dips, sell for profit” system on Solana, starting in a way that actually gets you to a working bot without getting bogged down in DEX internals on day one, while still leaving a clean path to optimize into Pump AMM / direct pool execution later.

The chain is Solana. The first execution path is Jupiter for swaps because it lets you ship an MVP quickly with fewer moving parts, and it gives you robust routing across liquidity venues when you’re still validating whether your dip logic even has an edge after fees and slippage. At the same time, the market-data layer will not be “Jupiter quotes” because those are too slow and rate-limited for high-fidelity dip detection; instead it will be on-chain pool monitoring for whichever venues you care about most (starting with the ones that cover your universe). The “Pump AMM” specialization will be a second-stage execution backend that replaces or complements Jupiter once the bot is stable and the signal is proven.

The signal logic I would implement first is deliberately simple but not naive. You’ll compute a price stream per market from on-chain sources and maintain a rolling 60-second window. A “dip” event is when the current price is at least 5% below a reference price in that window, but it won’t trigger purely on the price delta; it also requires minimum liquidity, a minimum amount of recent volume, and a maximum estimated slippage for your intended order size. The reason is straightforward: on thin pools, a 5% move is often just noise or manipulation and you’ll become exit liquidity. So the signal is “price drop threshold + market quality gates,” not just “price drop.” The first exit logic is also mechanical: take-profit at a small percent above entry (or at a mean reversion back toward the window reference), stop-loss at a defined adverse move, and a time stop to avoid bag-holding when reversion doesn’t come. This is a scalp system, so it must be willing to get out quickly and accept small losses.

Architecturally, I would build this as one process to start (to reduce complexity and make iteration fast), but designed as clean internal modules with service-like boundaries so you can split them later if you scale. Internally you’ll have: a MarketData module that maintains live state and rolling windows; a SignalEngine that evaluates dip conditions and produces trade intents; a RiskManager that approves/rejects intents based on exposure and safety rules; an ExecutionEngine that turns approved intents into swaps, simulates, submits, confirms, and records fills; and a Portfolio/PositionTracker that maintains truth about what you hold and what PnL you’ve realized. Around all of that you’ll have Observability (structured logging + metrics) and Persistence (a small database) so you can reproduce decisions and debug failures.

The MarketData module is the foundation. It subscribes over WebSockets to the accounts that define price for your monitored markets. For AMMs, that’s typically the pool reserve accounts (base and quote vaults) plus any extra accounts needed for the pool state. From reserve balances you derive spot price and compute instantaneous price changes without relying on third-party charts. The module maintains a per-market rolling window in memory (ring buffer) of (timestamp, price, reserves, derived liquidity, derived volatility, recent volume proxy). It also tracks health: how fresh the last update is, whether the subscription is lagging, and whether data is internally consistent. If MarketData is stale, nothing else trades—this is how you avoid trading blind.

The SignalEngine runs on every market update. It computes the 60-second reference price (either the oldest price in the window or a robust statistic like a median/EMA to reduce sensitivity) and checks whether the current price is below that by your threshold. It then applies gates: liquidity must exceed a threshold; the implied price impact for your order size must be below a threshold; and the market must not be in cooldown from a recent trade. You can add additional gates later, like avoiding tokens with suspicious authorities or obvious rug patterns, but the MVP gates are the ones that directly prevent “5% dip” from being a trap in shallow pools. The output of the SignalEngine is a TradeIntent object that includes the market, side (buy), size, and “reason codes” (which thresholds were met). Those reason codes matter a lot for debugging and learning.

The RiskManager sits between “signal” and “execution.” It enforces your non-negotiables: max SOL spent per trade, max concurrent open positions, max exposure per token, max daily loss, and a global kill switch. It also enforces a policy that you never average down automatically and you never open a new position while the system is in an unhealthy state (stale market data, RPC degraded, high failure rate). If an intent fails a risk check, it is rejected and logged with the exact reason. That audit trail is how you improve.

The ExecutionEngine is where you start with Jupiter for swaps. The execution flow is: request a fresh route/quote for the intended swap size; compute min-out using your slippage tolerance (dynamic, based on liquidity/volatility); build the transaction; attach compute budget configuration for priority fee; simulate; submit; await confirmation; and record the fill. The important part is that execution is stateful and idempotent: once you decide to buy, you track that trade attempt with an ID so retries don’t double-buy. If simulation fails, you bail immediately. If submission fails due to transient network errors, you retry with bounded attempts and optionally slightly higher priority fee, but you do not spam. You also implement multi-RPC failover: one connection is used for streaming subscriptions, and one or more are used for transaction submission. If one submission RPC degrades, you route around it.

The PositionTracker is a state machine. A position doesn’t jump from “none” to “owned” instantly because confirmations are asynchronous. Positions have states like PENDING_OPEN, OPEN, PENDING_CLOSE, CLOSED, FAILED. Your exits are driven by the same MarketData stream, but filtered through safety rules. When OPEN, you compute unrealized PnL using the same price source as your signal. When an exit condition triggers, you create a sell intent and send it through RiskManager (yes, exits also go through risk checks to avoid selling into impossible conditions without acknowledging it). You then execute the sell with the same simulate/submit/confirm pipeline. Every transition is logged and persisted.

Persistence is minimal but essential. You store: trade intents, approvals/rejections, executed transactions and signatures, fills, position lifecycle events, and snapshots of key market features at decision time (price, liquidity, window stats). This allows replay and analysis. For MVP you can use SQLite locally; if you deploy to a server and want durability, move to Postgres. You don’t need Kafka or heavy queues at the beginning; internal in-memory queues and a small DB are enough.

Once that MVP works and is stable, you decide whether the execution backend needs to become “direct Pump AMM.” This is where you replace the Jupiter execution adapter with a Pump-AMM adapter that builds swap instructions directly against the Pump program. You do this only after you have: proven signal edge under realistic simulation, proven reliability, and built strong risk controls. The direct adapter gives you lower latency and more control, but it increases engineering complexity because you must decode pool state precisely and maintain compatibility with program changes. The key is that your architecture already isolates execution behind an interface, so you’re swapping an adapter, not rewriting the bot.

The implementation order is important. You first build MarketData with a single market and prove your derived price changes correctly in real time. You then add rolling windows and the dip detection logic and log dip events without trading. Next you add a paper-trading execution mode that records what it would have done and what the outcome would have been using live data, again without real transactions. Then you add the real ExecutionEngine with tiny size and strict risk limits. Only once the system runs for long periods without crashing, missing data, or misbehaving do you expand the token/pool universe and tune thresholds. Finally, if you still want to emulate the Pump AMM wallet specifically, you add the Pump direct adapter and restrict it to Pump markets where your monitoring and safety checks are strongest.

That’s the plan: Solana chain, MVP execution via Jupiter, market data via on-chain pool subscriptions, signal as “60s drop threshold + liquidity/impact gates,” exits as take-profit/stop-loss/time-stop, modular architecture with MarketData → SignalEngine → RiskManager → ExecutionEngine → PositionTracker, full simulation-before-send, RPC failover, and persistent audit logging. It’s specific enough to build, and it’s structured so you can later specialize to Pump AMM without throwing everything away.
